Education Access and Household Wellbeing Across Malaysian Districts

Household wellbeing is shaped by more than income alone. This visualisation compares Malaysian districts using three connected dimensions: household income, access to basic amenities, and education access measured through school density.

The analysis uses 2022 as the latest common district-level comparison year across the selected datasets. The aim is not only to identify the districts that appear strongest overall, but also to show where economic advantage, service access, and educational provision do not fully align.

Method note: the HDX administrative boundary file contains fewer districts than the cleaned tabular dataset, so a small number of district values do not appear in the maps. The ranked and state-level charts remain unaffected by this boundary mismatch.

1. The geography of household wellbeing

The opening section establishes where household income is strongest. The map gives the national overview, while the ranked charts show how far the upper end of the income distribution is concentrated in a limited set of districts.

Takeaway: Higher-income districts cluster around the Klang Valley, Johor Bahru, and a small number of economically distinctive districts such as Bintulu.

Takeaway: The top-income ranking is dominated by major urban and industrial centres rather than a broad spread across all states.

Takeaway: State averages reduce the influence of exceptional districts and highlight which states perform well more consistently across their districts.

2. Looking beyond raw counts

School counts alone can be misleading because large and heavily populated districts naturally contain more institutions. A better comparison is school density, which adjusts school provision for population size and reveals where district patterns change.

Takeaway: After trimming extreme outliers, the relationship between school density and household income appears weak to moderate rather than strongly linear.

Takeaway: School density follows a different geography from income, showing that educational provision and economic strength do not always move together.

Takeaway: A small group of sparsely populated districts produce extremely high school-density values and would dominate an unfiltered comparison.

3. Where basic services still lag

Basic amenities are generally high across Malaysia, but weaker districts still matter because they reveal where the most basic living conditions remain uneven. The final section focuses on the clearest remaining gaps and then closes with a state-level summary.

Takeaway: The weakest piped-water districts are concentrated in Sabah, Sarawak, and a small number of outliers elsewhere, suggesting that basic service gaps are geographically uneven.

Takeaway: The composite score gives a broad state-level summary, but the detailed district charts remain necessary because state averages can hide important internal variation.
